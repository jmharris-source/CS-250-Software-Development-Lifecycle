CS 250 – Software Development Lifecycle

Sprint Review and Retrospective

Project Overview

In CS 250, I studied the Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) and deepened my understanding of Agile methodologies, especially Scrum. For the final project, I participated in developing SNHU Travel, a travel application for ChadaTech. This experience allowed me to take on various Scrum roles—Product Owner, Developer, Scrum Master, and Tester—and understand how each role supports successful product delivery.

A key takeaway from this course is that software development extends beyond coding. It requires understanding user needs, communicating effectively, adapting, and collaborating to deliver a product that meets expectations.

How Do I Interpret User Needs and implement them into a Program? How Does Creating User Stories Help With This?

Understanding actual user needs is essential in software development; relying on assumptions is risky. Before development, I gather requirements, ask questions, and identify the problems the application should solve. Since users may not always articulate technical needs, developers must translate their expectations into functional requirements.

User stories bridge this gap by letting developers see the application from the user's perspective. They clarify who needs a feature, what they aim to achieve, and why it is important.

In the SNHU Travel project, I saw the value of this process when the Product Owner requested detox and wellness travel options. This required updates to the destination slideshow. By understanding the purpose of the request, I ensured the new functionality met user expectations.

I also learned that acceptance criteria are as important as user stories. They set clear expectations and help developers and testers verify that features work as intended. I plan to use user stories and acceptance criteria to ensure my applications are both functional and meaningful.

How Do I Approach Developing Programs? What Agile Processes Do I Hope to Incorporate Into My Future Development Work?

I begin software development by clarifying the project's purpose, requirements, and expected outcomes. Breaking larger projects into smaller, manageable tasks keeps the process organized and on track.

Before this course, I valued planning and structured processes. Working with Scrum showed me that flexibility matters just as much. Requirements may change, challenges can arise, and feedback often leads to product improvements.

I plan to incorporate sprint planning into my future work, setting realistic goals and focusing on specific tasks each sprint. Maintaining a product backlog also provides visibility into priorities and keeps the team organized.

I also plan to use sprint retrospectives to evaluate both accomplishments and areas for improvement. Continuous improvement matters, as there are always opportunities to learn and improve team processes.

I intend to incorporate regular testing, feedback, and communication throughout development rather than waiting until the end of a project. The SNHU Travel assignments demonstrated how small requirement changes impact development and testing, highlighting the need for adaptability.

As I work toward becoming a software engineer, I aim to develop applications by combining technical expertise, organization, collaboration, and continuous improvement.

What Does It Mean to Be a Good Team Member in Software Development?

Being a good team member in software development means being dependable, communicating openly, respecting diverse perspectives, and recognizing that everyone contributes to the project's success.

Technical skills matter, but they aren't enough on their own. A developer may write excellent code, but without effective communication, openness to feedback, and collaboration, the entire team can be impacted negatively.

This course clarified how Scrum roles depend on each other. The Product Owner communicates priorities and user expectations, the Scrum Master guides the team and addresses obstacles, and Developers collaborate to deliver a functional product. Testing and quality assurance are also essential to meeting acceptance criteria.

I value accountability. If I commit to a task, I am responsible for completing it and communicating any obstacles. Good team members also help others, share knowledge, and remain open to constructive criticism.

My background in information technology taught me that troubleshooting and problem-solving require patience, communication, and teamwork. This course reinforced these lessons and demonstrated their relevance to Agile software development.

A successful development team is built on trust, transparency, shared responsibility, and a commitment to continuous improvement.

Final Reflection

CS 250 improved my understanding of how software projects progress from initial requirements to completion. Through the SNHU Travel project, I gained experience interpreting user needs, developing user stories, applying acceptance criteria, adapting to changing requirements, and recognizing the importance of communication within a Scrum Team.

My biggest takeaway is that successful software development requires more than technical ability. It requires understanding people, remaining flexible, and working toward a common goal.

As I continue my Computer Science degree with a concentration in Software Engineering, I plan to apply these lessons to future coursework, personal projects, and eventually my professional career as a software engineer.
